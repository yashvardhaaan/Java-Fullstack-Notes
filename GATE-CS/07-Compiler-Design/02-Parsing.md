# 07 — COMPILER DESIGN

## 02 — Parsing — GATE CSE Notes

> **GATE Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ VERY HIGH**
>
> Parsing is one of the **most important Compiler Design topics**. GATE frequently asks about **FIRST/FOLLOW, LL(1), LR parsing, shift-reduce conflicts, SLR/LR(1)/LALR, parse trees, ambiguity, and grammar transformations**.

---

# 1. What is Parsing?

Parsing is the process of checking whether the sequence of tokens generated by the lexical analyzer follows the grammar of the programming language.

```text
Source Code
     ↓
Lexical Analyzer
     ↓
Tokens
     ↓
Parser
     ↓
Parse Tree / Syntax Tree
```

### Example

Grammar:

$$
E\rightarrow E+T\mid T
$$

Input:

```text
id + id
```

Parser determines whether this token sequence can be generated by the grammar.

---

# 2. Input and Output

### Input

$$
\boxed{\text{Token stream}}
$$

### Output

Usually:

$$
\boxed{\text{Parse Tree}}
$$

or an abstract syntax tree.

---

# 3. Two Major Types of Parsing

The biggest classification:

```text
                    PARSING
                       |
             ┌─────────┴─────────┐
             ↓                   ↓
          Top-Down           Bottom-Up
             |                   |
       Leftmost derivation   Rightmost derivation
                              in reverse
```

---

# 4. Top-Down Parsing

Starts from the **start symbol** and tries to derive the input.

```text
Start Symbol
     ↓
   ...
     ↓
Input
```

It constructs the parse tree from:

$$
\boxed{\text{Root → Leaves}}
$$

### Examples

* Recursive descent
* Predictive parsing
* LL(1)

---

# 5. Bottom-Up Parsing

Starts from the input and reduces it toward the start symbol.

```text
Input
  ↓
 ...
  ↓
Start Symbol
```

It constructs the parse tree from:

$$
\boxed{\text{Leaves → Root}}
$$

### Examples

* Shift-reduce parsing
* LR(0)
* SLR(1)
* CLR(1)
* LALR(1)

---

# 6. 🔥 GATE Trick

Remember:

### Top-down

$$
\boxed{\text{Leftmost derivation}}
$$

### Bottom-up

$$
\boxed{\text{Rightmost derivation in reverse}}
$$

This is a very common conceptual question.

---

# 7. Leftmost vs Rightmost Derivation

Consider:

$$
S\rightarrow AB
$$

$$
A\rightarrow a
$$

$$
B\rightarrow b
$$

### Leftmost derivation

Always expand the **leftmost non-terminal**.

$$
S\Rightarrow AB
$$

$$
\Rightarrow aB
$$

$$
\Rightarrow ab
$$

---

### Rightmost derivation

Always expand the **rightmost non-terminal**.

$$
S\Rightarrow AB
$$

$$
\Rightarrow Ab
$$

$$
\Rightarrow ab
$$

---

# 8. Ambiguous Grammar

A grammar is ambiguous if there exists at least one string that has:

$$
\boxed{\text{More than one parse tree}}
$$

Equivalently, it has:

$$
\boxed{\text{More than one leftmost derivation}}
$$

or:

$$
\boxed{\text{More than one rightmost derivation}}
$$

---

# 9. Classic Ambiguous Grammar

Consider:

$$
E\rightarrow E+E\mid E*E\mid id
$$

For:

$$
id+id*id
$$

there can be two interpretations:

```text
(id + id) * id
```

or:

```text
id + (id * id)
```

Therefore the grammar is ambiguous.

---

# 10. Removing Ambiguity

We can encode precedence using separate non-terminals:

$$
E\rightarrow E+T\mid T
$$

$$
T\rightarrow T*F\mid F
$$

$$
F\rightarrow (E)\mid id
$$

Now:

$$
*\quad\text{has higher precedence than}\quad+
$$

---

# 11. Associativity

Consider:

$$
a-b-c
$$

### Left associative

$$
(a-b)-c
$$

### Right associative

$$
a-(b-c)
$$

---

## GATE Trick

Left recursion generally gives **left associativity**.

Example:

$$
E\rightarrow E+T\mid T
$$

gives:

$$
a+b+c
$$

as:

$$
(a+b)+c
$$

---

# 12. Left Recursion

A grammar is left recursive if:

$$
A\Rightarrow^+A\alpha
$$

Immediate left recursion:

$$
\boxed{A\rightarrow A\alpha\mid\beta}
$$

### Example

$$
E\rightarrow E+T\mid T
$$

This is left recursive.

---

# 13. Removing Immediate Left Recursion

Given:

$$
A\rightarrow A\alpha\mid\beta
$$

convert to:

$$
\boxed{A\rightarrow\beta A'}
$$

$$
\boxed{A'\rightarrow\alpha A'\mid\epsilon}
$$

### Example

$$
E\rightarrow E+T\mid T
$$

becomes:

$$
E\rightarrow TE'
$$

$$
E'\rightarrow +TE'\mid\epsilon
$$

---

# 14. Why Remove Left Recursion?

Top-down parsers such as recursive-descent parsers can enter infinite recursion.

Example:

```text
E()
 ↓
E()
 ↓
E()
 ↓
...
```

Therefore left recursion must be removed for standard predictive top-down parsing.

---

# 15. Left Factoring

Used when alternatives have a common prefix.

Suppose:

$$
A\rightarrow\alpha\beta_1\mid\alpha\beta_2
$$

Factor:

$$
\boxed{A\rightarrow\alpha A'}
$$

$$
\boxed{A'\rightarrow\beta_1\mid\beta_2}
$$

---

### Example

$$
S\rightarrow if\ E\ then\ S\ else\ S
$$

$$
S\rightarrow if\ E\ then\ S
$$

Common prefix:

```text
if E then S
```

Left factoring may be required for predictive parsing.

---

# 16. FIRST Set

The **FIRST** set of a grammar symbol contains terminals that can appear as the **first symbol** of strings derived from it.

$$
\boxed{FIRST(\alpha)}
$$

= terminals that can begin strings derived from `α`.

---

### Example

$$
A\rightarrow ab
$$

Then:

$$
FIRST(A)=\{a\}
$$

---

# 17. FIRST with Epsilon

Suppose:

$$
A\rightarrow\epsilon
$$

Then:

$$
\boxed{\epsilon\in FIRST(A)}
$$

---

Suppose:

$$
A\rightarrow BC
$$

Then:

$$
FIRST(A)
$$

starts with:

$$
FIRST(B)
$$

but if:

$$
\epsilon\in FIRST(B)
$$

then we also consider:

$$
FIRST(C)
$$

---

# 18. FOLLOW Set

FOLLOW(A) contains terminals that can appear immediately after `A` in some sentential form.

### Golden rule

For the start symbol:

$$
\boxed{\$\in FOLLOW(S)}
$$

where `$` represents end of input.

---

# 19. FOLLOW Rules

For:

$$
A\rightarrow\alpha B\beta
$$

add:

$$
FIRST(\beta)-\{\epsilon\}
$$

to:

$$
FOLLOW(B)
$$

If:

$$
\beta\Rightarrow^*\epsilon
$$

then:

$$
FOLLOW(A)\subseteq FOLLOW(B)
$$

---

# 20. Example FIRST/FOLLOW

Grammar:

$$
S\rightarrow AB
$$

$$
A\rightarrow a\mid\epsilon
$$

$$
B\rightarrow b
$$

### FIRST

$$
FIRST(A)=\{a,\epsilon\}
$$

$$
FIRST(B)=\{b\}
$$

Because `A` can disappear:

$$
FIRST(S)=\{a,b\}
$$

---

### FOLLOW

Since `S` is start:

$$
FOLLOW(S)=\{\$\}
$$

Since `B` is at the end:

$$
FOLLOW(B)=\{\$\}
$$

Since `A` is followed by `B`:

$$
FOLLOW(A)=\{b\}
$$

---

# 21. LL(1) Parsing

LL(1) means:

### First L

Input is scanned:

$$
\boxed{\text{Left → Right}}
$$

### Second L

Produces:

$$
\boxed{\text{Leftmost derivation}}
$$

### 1

Uses:

$$
\boxed{\text{1 lookahead symbol}}
$$

---

# 22. LL(1) Grammar Condition

For alternatives:

$$
A\rightarrow\alpha\mid\beta
$$

we need:

$$
FIRST(\alpha)\cap FIRST(\beta)=\emptyset
$$

for straightforward predictive parsing.

If epsilon is involved, FOLLOW also matters.

If:

$$
\epsilon\in FIRST(\alpha)
$$

then:

$$
FIRST(\beta)\cap FOLLOW(A)=\emptyset
$$

---

# 23. LL(1) Parsing Table

For:

$$
A\rightarrow\alpha
$$

put the production in table entry:

$$
M[A,a]
$$

for every:

$$
a\in FIRST(\alpha)
$$

If:

$$
\epsilon\in FIRST(\alpha)
$$

put it for every:

$$
a\in FOLLOW(A)
$$

---

# 24. 🔥 GATE Shortcut

### LL(1) conflict

If one table cell contains:

$$
\boxed{\text{more than one production}}
$$

then the grammar is **not LL(1)**.

---

# 25. Recursive Descent

A recursive-descent parser uses a set of recursive procedures.

Typically:

```text
E() → parses E
T() → parses T
F() → parses F
```

It is a top-down technique.

---

# 26. Predictive Parser

A predictive parser chooses the correct production using lookahead without backtracking.

LL(1) is a major example.

```text
Lookahead
    ↓
Parsing Table
    ↓
Production
```

---

# 27. Shift-Reduce Parsing

Bottom-up parsers use:

$$
\boxed{\text{Shift}}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{\text{Reduce}}
$$

### Shift

Move the next input symbol onto the stack.

### Reduce

Replace a handle on the stack by the corresponding non-terminal.

---

# 28. Four Basic Actions

LR parser actions:

```text
SHIFT
REDUCE
ACCEPT
ERROR
```

---

# 29. Shift Operation

Input:

```text
id + id
```

The parser may shift:

```text
id
```

onto the stack.

```text
Stack       Input
id          + id
```

---

# 30. Reduce Operation

If grammar contains:

$$
F\rightarrow id
$$

then:

```text
id
```

can be reduced to:

```text
F
```

---

# 31. Handle

A **handle** is a substring that corresponds to the RHS of a production and whose reduction represents one step in the reverse of a rightmost derivation.

### GATE memory

$$
\boxed{\text{Bottom-up parsing finds handles}}
$$

---

# 32. LR Parsing

LR means:

### L

Scan input:

$$
\boxed{\text{Left → Right}}
$$

### R

Construct:

$$
\boxed{\text{Rightmost derivation in reverse}}
$$

---

# 33. LR Family

Very important:

```text
LR(0)
SLR(1)
LALR(1)
CLR(1) / Canonical LR(1)
```

Generally:

$$
\boxed{LR(0)\subseteq SLR(1)\subseteq LALR(1)\subseteq CLR(1)}
$$

in terms of grammar-class power.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{CLR(1)\text{ is the most powerful among these}}
$$

---

# 34. Parser Power

Remember:

$$
\boxed{LR(0)<SLR(1)<LALR(1)<CLR(1)}
$$

More powerful parsers can handle a larger class of grammars.

---

# 35. LR(0)

LR(0) items do **not** use lookahead symbols.

Example:

$$
A\rightarrow XYZ
$$

Possible LR(0) items:

$$
A\rightarrow\cdot XYZ
$$

$$
A\rightarrow X\cdot YZ
$$

$$
A\rightarrow XY\cdot Z
$$

$$
A\rightarrow XYZ\cdot
$$

---

# 36. Meaning of Dot

For:

$$
A\rightarrow X\cdot YZ
$$

the parser has recognized:

```text
X
```

and expects:

```text
Y Z
```

### Dot at beginning

$$
A\rightarrow\cdot XYZ
$$

Nothing recognized yet.

### Dot at end

$$
A\rightarrow XYZ\cdot
$$

Entire RHS recognized → possible reduction.

---

# 37. SLR(1)

SLR uses:

$$
\boxed{LR(0)\text{ items + FOLLOW sets}}
$$

For a completed item:

$$
A\rightarrow\alpha\cdot
$$

reduce using:

$$
FOLLOW(A)
$$

---

# 38. CLR(1)

Canonical LR(1) uses:

$$
\boxed{\text{LR(1) items with lookahead}}
$$

An item looks like:

$$
[A\rightarrow\alpha\cdot\beta,\ a]
$$

where `a` is a lookahead terminal.

CLR is more powerful than SLR.

---

# 39. LALR(1)

LALR combines CLR states having the same LR(0) core.

### Key idea

```text
CLR(1) states
      ↓
merge states with same LR(0) core
      ↓
LALR(1)
```

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{LALR has fewer states than CLR}}
$$

in the usual construction.

---

# 40. 🔥 GATE Comparison

| Parser  | Main idea                                        |
| ------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| LR(0)   | No lookahead                                     |
| SLR(1)  | FOLLOW-based reduction                           |
| LALR(1) | Merge compatible CLR states with same LR(0) core |
| CLR(1)  | Full LR(1) lookahead                             |

---

# 41. Shift-Reduce Conflict

Occurs when parser can either:

```text
SHIFT
```

or:

```text
REDUCE
```

for the same situation.

$$
\boxed{\text{Shift/Reduce Conflict}}
$$

---

# 42. Reduce-Reduce Conflict

Occurs when parser can choose between:

```text
Reduce by A → α
```

and:

```text
Reduce by B → β
```

for the same situation.

$$
\boxed{\text{Reduce/Reduce Conflict}}
$$

---

# 43. Which Conflict is More Serious?

Both indicate a parsing-table problem.

But in practice:

$$
\boxed{\text{Reduce/Reduce conflict is generally more problematic}}
$$

because there are two competing reductions.

---

# 44. Dangling Else

Classic ambiguity:

```c id="8m3k8q"
if E then
    if E then
        S
    else
        S
```

Which `if` does `else` belong to?

Usually:

$$
\boxed{\text{else binds to nearest unmatched if}}
$$

This is called the **dangling-else problem**.

---

# 45. Operator Precedence

For:

$$
a+b*c
$$

if `*` has higher precedence:

$$
\boxed{a+(b*c)}
$$

not:

$$
(a+b)*c
$$

Parser grammar can encode precedence using different non-terminals.

---

# 46. Associativity Trick

For left associative `+`:

$$
E\rightarrow E+T\mid T
$$

For right associative operator, grammar is typically structured differently.

### GATE shortcut

```text
Left recursion → usually left associative
Right recursion → often right associative
```

"Usually" matters because associativity ultimately depends on grammar/semantic specification.

---

# 47. Ambiguity vs Left Recursion

Do not confuse them.

### Left recursion

A structural property of grammar:

$$
A\Rightarrow^+A\alpha
$$

### Ambiguity

A string has multiple parse trees/derivations.

A grammar can be:

* left recursive but unambiguous
* ambiguous without being immediately left recursive

---

# 48. Parser Comparison

| Property               | LL(1)                | LR(1)                |
| ---------------------- | -------------------- | -------------------- |
| Direction              | Top-down             | Bottom-up            |
| Derivation             | Leftmost             | Rightmost in reverse |
| Lookahead              | 1                    | 1                    |
| Left recursion         | Not allowed directly | Can handle           |
| Grammar power          | Lower                | Higher               |
| Typical implementation | Predictive           | Shift-reduce         |

---

# 49. 🔥 Very Important GATE Ordering

Memorize:

$$
\boxed{LL(1)\subseteq LR(0)\subseteq SLR(1)\subseteq LALR(1)\subseteq CLR(1)}
$$

This is a useful **strict-power intuition** for standard grammar classes, though exact inclusions/relationships can depend on the class definition being discussed.

---

# 50. Common GATE Traps

### ❌ Trap 1

"LL parser uses rightmost derivation."

False.

$$
\boxed{LL\rightarrow Leftmost}
$$

---

### ❌ Trap 2

"LR means left-to-right derivation."

False.

LR means:

$$
\boxed{\text{Left-to-right scanning + Rightmost derivation in reverse}}
$$

---

### ❌ Trap 3

"LALR has more states than CLR."

False.

LALR is formed by merging CLR states.

$$
\boxed{|LALR\ states|\le|CLR\ states|}
$$

---

### ❌ Trap 4

"SLR uses LR(1) items."

Not exactly.

SLR uses:

$$
\boxed{LR(0)\ items + FOLLOW}
$$

---

### ❌ Trap 5

"Removing left recursion makes any grammar LL(1)."

False.

You may also need **left factoring**, and other conflicts can remain.

# 51. PYQ Pattern — FIRST/FOLLOW

Given a grammar such as:

$$
S\rightarrow AB
$$

$$
A\rightarrow a\mid\epsilon
$$

$$
B\rightarrow b\mid\epsilon
$$

GATE may ask:

* `FIRST(S)`
* `FOLLOW(A)`
* `FOLLOW(B)`
* Whether grammar is LL(1)

### Solution

Because both `A` and `B` can derive epsilon:

$$
FIRST(S)=\{a,b,\epsilon\}
$$

And:

$$
FOLLOW(S)=\{\$\}
$$

Since `A` is followed by `B`:

$$
FOLLOW(A)=\{b,\$\}
$$

because `B` can derive ε.

---

# 52. PYQ Pattern — Remove Left Recursion

Given:

$$
E\rightarrow E+T\mid T
$$

Answer:

$$
\boxed{E\rightarrow TE'}
$$

$$
\boxed{E'\rightarrow +TE'\mid\epsilon}
$$

This transformation is extremely important.

---

# 53. PYQ Pattern — Parser Identification

Question may ask:

> Which parser constructs the rightmost derivation in reverse?

Answer:

$$
\boxed{LR}
$$

---

# 54. PYQ Pattern — LR Family

Question:

> Which parser has the greatest power?

Usually:

$$
\boxed{CLR(1)}
$$

among:

```text
LR(0)
SLR(1)
LALR(1)
CLR(1)
```

---

# 55. PYQ Pattern — State Count

A common GATE-style question gives a grammar and asks the number of states in:

* LR(0)
* SLR
* LALR
* CLR

### Important

SLR uses the **same LR(0) automaton states** as LR(0); SLR changes the reduction decisions using FOLLOW sets.

LALR merges CLR states with identical LR(0) cores.

---

# 56. PYQ Pattern — Conflict

Given a parsing table:

```text
ACTION[state, token] =
SHIFT
REDUCE
```

simultaneously.

Answer:

$$
\boxed{\text{Shift-Reduce Conflict}}
$$

If:

```text
REDUCE A
REDUCE B
```

then:

$$
\boxed{\text{Reduce-Reduce Conflict}}
$$
